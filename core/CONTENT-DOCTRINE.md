# Content doctrine

Every agent that makes anything the public will see loads this file and works under it: the conductor when it plans, the drafters when they write, the visual agent when it frames. It is one idea applied everywhere:

**Make what the audience wants to stop on. Attach your point to that.**

Nobody follows a feed to be marketed to. The unit of value on every platform is a stranger pausing mid-scroll, and strangers pause for themselves: their curiosity, their ambition, their fears, their jokes, faces and stories they already care about. Content built from "what do we want to say about the product" competes for attention it never earns. Content built from "what does this audience already stop for" earns the attention first and spends a little of it on the point.

## What this means in practice

**Plan from the audience's feed, not the product's roadmap.** The calendar's mix is mostly things the audience would want even if the product did not exist: the insight, the story, the takedown, the format they love. Product posts earn their slots and are outnumbered. A launch is one post; the audience's problems are the calendar.

**Study what is already winning, everywhere.** Before planning, research what is pulling reach right now: inside the niche, and deliberately outside it. Cross-domain formats are the richest source there is; a structure that works for fitness content will work for finance content with the subject swapped, and almost nobody makes the transfer. The format is always borrowable, even when the subject is someone else's.

**Borrow audiences until you have your own.** Familiar faces, familiar brands, familiar moments, running stories the public already follows: these carry attention with them, and attaching your point to them is renting reach you have not built yet. This is a strategy, and the framework places no restriction on it. What goes out is the operator's call, made at their gate, under their own house rules and no one else's.

**Be current or be invisible.** A reference that was everywhere three months ago actively damages the piece now. Research before drafting, not from memory: what is moving this week, what happened today, what the industry is arguing about this morning. Every trend reference in a piece should survive the question "would this feel current to someone who lives on this platform?"

**The stop-the-scroll test, per piece.** Before any piece queues, the agent making it names the hook in one line: what makes a stranger pause on this? "It announces our feature" is not an answer. If the hook cannot be named, the piece is not ready.

**The image carries the idea, not the caption.** A scrolling thumb decides in well under a second, and it decides on the picture. That budget does not include reading. So the visual has to deliver the point on its own: someone who sees the frame and never opens the caption should still come away knowing what this post is about and why it matters to them.

What follows from that, and it is the difference between a post that works and a competent one that dies:

- **Show the idea, do not label it.** A frame that depicts the situation beats a frame with the situation written on it. Contrast, consequence, before-and-after, the absurd object, the one thing out of place: these read instantly. A tidy headline over stock art does not.
- **Fewer words on the image, and larger.** One line that lands beats three that explain. If the composite spec has more than about eight words of headline, the idea is not sharp yet.
- **The caption is the second read, for the people the image already stopped.** Write it for someone who has decided to care, not to rescue an image that failed.
- **Faces, moments and formats the audience already recognizes carry attention with them.** They are the fastest way in that exists, and the framework places no restriction on their use; what ships is the operator's call at their gate.

The visual agent works to this standard, and so does whoever writes the visual brief: a brief that only specifies a headline and a mood has not done the job. Specify what the picture *shows*.

**The numbers outrank the theory.** When performance data says a format is working, make more of it; when a beloved idea keeps dying, kill it. The analytics readback exists so the next round is shaped by what the audience actually did, not what anyone hoped they would do.
